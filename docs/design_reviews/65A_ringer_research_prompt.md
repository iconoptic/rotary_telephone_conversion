# Deep research request: identify the "65A" bell ringer and reconcile it with our drive design

## Your role

Act as two specialists working together:

1. A **telephone-collector/historian** who can identify vintage Bell System / independent-manufacturer ringer part numbers from a physical marking, and cite Bell System Practices (BSP), manufacturer catalogs, or telephone-collector reference material for its documented spec.
2. A **power-electronics engineer** who reconciles that spec against a DIY bipolar transformer drive already built and bench-measured, and explains a specific mechanical symptom (vibration without a clean clapper strike).

Show your work: cite sources for any factual claim about "65A", separate "documented spec" from "your inference", and flag anything you cannot verify as unconfirmed rather than presenting it as fact.

## What just happened (the trigger for this request)

While troubleshooting why the bell won't strike cleanly, the owner noticed **"65A" imprinted on top of the ringer's metal cap** — a marking not previously read or accounted for anywhere in this project's design work. Nothing about the drive electronics, target voltage/frequency, or expected coil impedance was ever chosen with a "65A" spec in mind — the whole design so far was based on generic "vintage polarized ringer, ~90V/20Hz" folklore (a prior internal design review flagged this exact figure as an unverified citation, "treat as folklore" — it was never traced to a primary source). If "65A" identifies a real, documented ringer type, its actual spec should now take priority over that folklore figure.

## What we already know (measured/observed, not guessed)

- **Coil resistance:** the ringer's RED/BLACK lead pair measures **5.97kΩ DC** (Fluke DMM). This is a real measurement, trust it over any datasheet nominal value if they conflict.
- **A second, unused lead pair exists:** the ringer also has two **GREY leads**, never connected in the original phone, contacts left insulated. Our working assumption (never verified against an actual ringer schematic) has been "on many ringers this is a coil tap, and bonding it would short part of the winding" — **but if "65A" is a biased ringer that needs a separate DC bias winding, or a specific series/parallel two-coil configuration, this GREY pair might be exactly that**, and our decision to leave it untouched could be the root cause of the weak-strike symptom below. This is the single most important thing to resolve.
- **Mechanical construction:** the ringer has two coils driving a polarized armature between two gongs (typical U.S. desk/wall-phone biased ringer), with a permanent-magnet-biased armature that must see field reversal to strike alternately — i.e., it needs true bipolar (AC) drive, not a one-directional pulse train. (This part is standard telephone-ringer theory, not specific to "65A" — but confirm it still applies once "65A" is identified.)
- **Our current drive (already built and bench-tested):** a push-pull step-up transformer (Hammond 160G24, dual 115V primaries wired in series = 19:1 step-up from a plain 5V logic rail) driving the RED/BLACK coil pair through a 220Ω 1W series resistor. Verified live: **~86V RMS unloaded** across RED/BLACK at 25Hz full square wave (measured with the bell disconnected); loaded current is estimated ~13–15mA from the coil resistance and series resistor, not directly measured. Frequency and on-time/duty have both been swept comprehensively (roughly 15–40Hz, and duty-limited "strike mode" on-times from ~1ms to ~9ms) — see `bell_sweep_test.py` in the repo root if useful context.
- **Symptom:** with the metal cap off, the armature visibly **vibrates** across this whole sweep, but the **clapper does not seem to make solid contact** with the gongs.

## Specific questions to answer

1. **Identify "65A"**: what telephone-industry component does a "65A" (or "65-A") marking on a bell/ringer cap typically denote? Which manufacturer(s)/era(s) use this exact designation (Western Electric, Automatic Electric, Stromberg-Carlson, or another)? Cite your source(s) — Bell System Practices document numbers, manufacturer catalogs, or reputable telephone-collector references (e.g. Telephone Collectors International, historical BSP archives) are preferred over forum posts. If multiple plausible matches exist, list them with your confidence and what additional marking/measurement would disambiguate them.
2. **Documented electrical spec**: for whichever match(es) you find, what is the documented coil DC resistance (single coil and/or total), rated ringing voltage, rated ringing frequency (20Hz vs 30Hz vs other historical exchange frequencies), and whether it is described as a **biased/polarized** ringer (needs AC reversal) or something else. Compare against our measured 5.97kΩ — does it match, and if not, what does the mismatch imply (e.g., we're measuring only one of two series/parallel coils)?
3. **The GREY lead pair — resolve this explicitly**: does this ringer type use a two-coil configuration where BOTH coil pairs must be connected (e.g., two coils in series for AC drive, or one coil pair for a DC biasing/holding function) for proper operation? If GREY is a bias winding, what should it be connected to (a DC source, tied to a specific rail, or does it exist for a different vintage-network reason like a local extension bell or a different ringing voltage tap) and is leaving it open likely to explain a weak/non-striking armature?
4. **Diagnose "vibrates but doesn't strike"**: given a biased polarized ringer being driven with a true bipolar square wave already confirmed present (~86V unloaded, ~13–15mA estimated) across the full 15–40Hz range and multiple duty cycles, is the most likely explanation (a) still insufficient current/force (needs more mA than we're delivering), (b) wrong drive frequency for this specific armature's mechanical resonance, (c) a missing/miswired bias element (see GREY pair above), or (d) a purely **mechanical** issue — armature air-gap, return-spring tension, or clapper-to-gong gap out of adjustment — that no amount of electrical tuning can fix? Rank these by likelihood given the evidence above, and explain what evidence would distinguish them (e.g., "if it's purely mechanical, a hand-held battery/wall-wart DC pulse test into one coil should produce one crisp, forceful clack — a weak/mushy clack even under strong forced DC narrows it to mechanical").
5. **Recommendation**: a concrete target (voltage, frequency, and wiring including the GREY pair) to try next, explicitly noting anything that would require exceeding our current build's safe limits (T1 secondary is a 19:1 step-up from 5V logic; Q2/Q3 MOSFETs are rated 60V; do not recommend a change that pushes peak drive voltage materially past that without flagging it clearly as requiring a hardware change, not just a firmware tweak). If mechanical adjustment is implicated, describe what to inspect/adjust on a typical biased-ringer armature assembly (return spring tension, armature-to-pole-piece air gap, clapper stroke/gong gap set screw if this ringer type has one) at a level a hobbyist with basic tools could act on.
